=== Context and thesis
--- A trainer and his students
kw: evening classes · New York · 1936
Dale Carnegie was born in 1888 to a poor farming family in Missouri. After a difficult start as a salesman and an actor, he began in 1912 to teach evening classes in public speaking in New York, for clerks, salespeople and managers.

He quickly noticed that his students didn't just need to learn how to speak. Above all, they needed to learn how to get along with others: their customers, colleagues, bosses and families. He looked for a practical handbook on the subject and found none. So he decided to write one.

For years he collected stories, read biographies of famous people, interviewed people who were successful in their relationships and, above all, had his students test his principles. Each week they had to apply a principle in their lives and report the result to the group. The book is the product of this living laboratory.

*How to Win Friends and Influence People*, published in 1936, was an immediate and lasting success. It has sold tens of millions of copies and remains one of the most widely read self-help books in the world. The title may sound manipulative today, but the content above all stresses sincerity and respect for others.

The book is organized in four parts: fundamental techniques for handling people, ways to make people like you, ways to win people to your way of thinking, and ways to lead without arousing resentment.

> À retenir | A handbook born of evening classes: every principle was tested by students in their real lives.

--- The thesis: relationships matter
kw: human skills · engineers · success
Carnegie starts from an observation. In most occupations, technical knowledge is not enough to succeed. An engineer, an accountant or a salesperson spends much of their time working with other people: persuading, cooperating, negotiating, resolving conflicts. Their success depends largely on their ability to do so.

He cites studies and accounts of the time suggesting that a large share of professional success depends on personality and the ability to lead others, more than on technical skills. These figures are hard to verify, but the general idea has been confirmed by much later research on the importance of interpersonal skills.

Carnegie asserts that these skills can be **learned**. No one is born charming or persuasive. You become so by practicing simple behaviors until they become habits.

He insists on one point, however: the book's techniques only work if they are **sincere**. A hypocritical compliment, feigned interest or a forced smile are quickly spotted and have the opposite effect. The book does not propose manipulating others, but changing your own attitude toward them.

Finally, he recommends a way of reading: slowly, rereading each chapter, noting the principles, applying them and regularly reviewing your progress.

- Interpersonal skills matter as much as technical skills.
- They are learned through practice.
- They only work if they are sincere.

=== Fundamental techniques
--- Don't criticize
kw: defensiveness · resentment · understanding
Carnegie's first principle is **don't criticize, condemn or complain**.

He explains that criticism is almost always futile. When you criticize someone, they become defensive and try to justify themselves. They don't change their behavior: they resent the person who criticized them. Carnegie notes that even the most hardened criminals often find justifications for their actions and consider themselves misunderstood. If they don't see themselves as guilty, a colleague or family member is unlikely to accept criticism easily.

He tells an anecdote about Abraham Lincoln. During the Civil War, a general had failed to seize a decisive opportunity to end the fighting. Lincoln, furious, wrote him a very harsh letter of reproach. But he never sent it. The letter was found after his death. Carnegie sees in it the wisdom of a man who had learned from experience that reproaches wound without improving anything.

Instead of criticism, Carnegie suggests **trying to understand**. Why did the person act this way? What were their constraints, reasons, intentions? Understanding doesn't mean accepting everything, but it changes how you react and makes a constructive conversation possible.

He stresses that this principle requires self-control. Criticizing is easy and brings immediate satisfaction. Understanding and forgiving take more effort.

> Question clé | What recent criticism did you make? Did it change the person's behavior, or only damage the relationship?

--- Honest appreciation
kw: importance · recognition · flattery
The second principle is to **give honest, sincere appreciation**.

Carnegie draws on an idea he attributes to thinkers such as the philosopher John Dewey and the psychologist William James: one of the deepest desires of human beings is to feel important, recognized and appreciated. This desire is almost as strong as basic needs. It explains many behaviors, from the most admirable to the most absurd.

Satisfying this desire in others is one of the most powerful ways to build good relationships. Yet most people rarely express appreciation. They notice mistakes but take good work for granted.

Carnegie tells the story of Charles Schwab, an early twentieth-century steel industry executive known for his ability to motivate workers. Schwab explained that his main talent was encouraging and recognizing others' efforts, and that he was sparing with criticism.

Carnegie carefully distinguishes **appreciation** from **flattery**. Flattery is false and self-serving: it tells people what they want to hear in order to get something. Appreciation is sincere: it recognizes something real and specific. Flattery is quickly unmasked. Sincere appreciation, on the other hand, is rare and precious.

He advises actively looking for others' qualities and achievements, noticing them and telling them. This exercise also changes the way you see them.

- Everyone wants to feel important and recognized.
- Sincere appreciation is rare; flattery is quickly spotted.
- Actively look for what deserves recognition.

--- Start from what the other person wants
kw: point of view · interest · creating desire
The third fundamental principle is to **arouse in the other person an eager want**, that is, to start from what the other person wants, not from what you want yourself.

Carnegie uses an image: when you go fishing, you don't bait the hook with what you like to eat, but with what the fish likes. Yet when we want to persuade someone, we almost always talk about what we want, our own needs, our own reasons.

He explains that the only lasting way to get someone to do something is to make them want to do it, by showing how it meets their own desires. That means putting yourself in their shoes and understanding what matters to them.

He gives many examples. A parent who wants a child to eat better gets more results by explaining that it will help them get stronger at play than by lecturing. A salesperson who talks about the customer's problems sells more than one who praises the product. An employee who asks for a raise by showing what they bring to the company is more convincing than one who talks about their own needs.

Carnegie quotes an idea attributed to Henry Ford: if there is any secret of success, it lies in the ability to get the other person's point of view and see things from their angle as well as from your own.

> Exercice | Before your next request, write down what the other person wants. Frame your request starting from their interests.

=== Making people like you
--- Be genuinely interested in others
kw: curiosity · attention · remembering
The second part of the book offers six ways to make people like you. The first is to **become genuinely interested in other people**.

Carnegie notes that most people try to get others interested in themselves: they talk about their successes, their problems, their opinions. But everyone is above all interested in themselves. So it is far more effective to take an interest in others than to try to impress them. He observes that you can make more friends in a few months by being interested in others than in a few years by trying to get them interested in you.

He gives several examples of famous people known for their attention to others. One of them knew the names and stories of the humblest employees around him and took the time to talk to them.

Taking an interest in others shows in simple gestures: asking about their lives, remembering what they told you, wishing them a happy birthday, checking in on them when they are going through a hard time. These gestures take time and attention, but they create strong relationships.

Carnegie again insists on sincerity. Feigned interest, aimed only at getting something, will be noticed. The point is to develop genuine curiosity about others, considering that every person has something interesting to teach.

> À retenir | You make more friends by being interested in others than by trying to get them interested in you.

--- The smile and the name
kw: welcome · name · respect
Two other principles in this part are very simple, but Carnegie attaches great importance to them.

**Smile.** A sincere smile tells the other person you are glad to see them. It immediately creates a favorable climate. Carnegie recounts that students in his course, asked to smile more for a week, noticed changes in their relationships at work and at home. He specifies that this is not a mechanical smile, but an inner attitude of goodwill. He adds that you can smile even on the phone: it can be heard in your voice.

**Remember people's names.** For everyone, their own name is one of the sweetest words to hear. Forgetting or mangling it is a small wound. Remembering and using it is a mark of respect and attention.

Carnegie tells the story of a famous industrialist of his time who, to win an important contract from a railroad company, named his new steel mill after that company's president. He also cites a politician known for his ability to remember the names of thousands of people, which had contributed to his popularity.

He suggests simple techniques for remembering names: ask the person to repeat it if you didn't hear it well, use the name several times in the conversation, associate it with an image or a detail, write it down after the meeting.

- A sincere smile opens the relationship.
- A person's name matters a great deal to them.
- Remembering a name takes a small deliberate effort.

--- Listen and talk about the other person
kw: active listening · their interests · importance
The last principles of this part concern conversation.

**Be a good listener and encourage others to talk about themselves.** Carnegie recounts an evening when he spent hours listening to a botanist talk about plants. At the end, the man told their host that Carnegie was a brilliant conversationalist. Yet Carnegie had said almost nothing: he had simply listened attentively and asked questions. He draws a lesson from this: people above all appreciate those who listen to them.

Really listening is rare. Most people are just waiting for their turn to speak. Listening means being attentive, not interrupting, asking questions that let the other person go further, and showing that you have understood.

**Talk in terms of the other person's interests.** Carnegie cites people who, before meeting someone, found out about their interests so they could talk about them. This is not manipulation, he explains, if the interest is sincere: it is a way of respecting the other person.

**Make the other person feel important, and do it sincerely.** He suggests looking for something to admire in everyone and saying so. He recounts how, at the post office, he decided to find something pleasant to say to a clerk who seemed bored. He noticed the man's fine head of hair and told him so. The clerk smiled, and the exchange changed tone.

> Exercice | In your next conversation, talk half as much as usual. Ask questions and listen to the answers all the way through.

=== Persuading without confrontation
--- Avoid arguments
kw: winning an argument · losing the person
The third part of the book offers ways to win people to your way of thinking. The first principle is surprising: **the only way to get the best of an argument is to avoid it**.

Carnegie says he learned this lesson the hard way. At a dinner, he corrected a guest who attributed a quotation to the Bible when it actually came from Shakespeare. A mutual friend, a Shakespeare expert, then sided against him in front of everyone. On the way home, the friend explained that he had knowingly sided with the other guest: what was the point of humiliating a guest over a detail?

Carnegie explains that you never really win an argument. If you lose it, you have lost. If you win it, the other person feels humiliated, and they don't change their mind: they resent the person who contradicted them. They remain convinced of what they thought.

He offers several tips for avoiding arguments: welcome disagreement as an opportunity to discover something, control your first defensive reaction, listen first, look for areas of agreement, be honest about your own mistakes, promise to think over the other person's ideas, and thank them for their interest.

The point is not to give up your ideas, but to understand that direct confrontation makes the other person less receptive.

> À retenir | Winning an argument means losing the other person's goodwill. Better to listen and look for agreement.

--- Respect the other person's opinion
kw: "you're wrong" · admitting mistakes
Carnegie then proposes two complementary principles.

**Show respect for other people's opinions, and never tell them they are wrong.** Telling someone they are wrong, whether by words, a look or a tone, amounts to attacking their intelligence and judgment. They defend themselves and become incapable of listening. Carnegie recommends instead cautious phrasing: I may see it differently, I may be wrong, let's examine the facts together. He cites Benjamin Franklin, who recounts in his autobiography how he learned to banish categorical assertions from his vocabulary and saw the benefits in his discussions.

**If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.** Carnegie recounts that he used to walk his dog off-leash in a park, which was forbidden. One day a policeman stopped him. Before the officer could speak, Carnegie admitted his fault, reproaching himself. The policeman, disarmed, became lenient. Carnegie explains that admitting your mistakes before the other person reproaches you removes their need to attack, and often prompts generosity.

He stresses that admitting you are wrong takes courage, but also brings a kind of relief. And it strengthens trust: we believe more readily someone who can admit their mistakes.

- Never say "you're wrong."
- Speak with caution rather than certainty.
- Admit your own mistakes quickly.

--- Get the other person saying yes
kw: friendly tone · yes · let them talk · their idea
Carnegie proposes a series of principles to make agreement easier.

**Begin in a friendly way.** A discussion that starts with hostility has little chance of succeeding. A discussion that starts with goodwill opens minds. Carnegie tells the story of an industrialist facing a strike who chose to address the strikers with respect and friendliness, and quickly found a way out of the conflict.

**Get "yes" responses right from the start.** Carnegie draws on the method attributed to Socrates, who asked questions to which his interlocutor could only answer yes, until leading him to a conclusion he would have rejected at the outset. Starting with points of agreement creates positive momentum.

**Let the other person do the talking.** Most people try to persuade by talking a lot. It is better to let the other person express their ideas, objections and needs. They feel heard, and they often reveal for themselves the arguments that will convince them.

**Let the other person feel the idea is theirs.** People hold more firmly to ideas they think they found themselves. Rather than imposing a solution, you can suggest leads and let the other person build their own conclusion.

**Try honestly to see things from the other person's point of view**, and **be sympathetic with their ideas and desires**.

> Question clé | In your last difficult discussion, how long did you talk, and how long did the other person talk?

--- Appeal to nobler motives
kw: generosity · dramatization · challenge
Carnegie closes this part with three principles.

**Appeal to the nobler motives.** Carnegie notes that people often act for two reasons: one that sounds good and the real one. But everyone likes to see themselves as honest, fair and generous. By appealing to these qualities, you encourage others to act in keeping with that image. He tells the story of a landlord who, faced with a tenant wanting to leave before the end of his lease, told him he considered him a man of his word. The tenant stayed.

**Dramatize your ideas.** An idea presented in a vivid, concrete way persuades more than an abstract explanation. Carnegie cites examples of salespeople and advertisers who demonstrated their products in spectacular ways.

**Throw down a challenge.** The desire to excel and succeed is a powerful driver. Carnegie tells the story of Charles Schwab who, in a steel mill where the crews were producing little, simply chalked on the floor the number of heats completed by the day shift. The night shift, stung, set out to do better, and production quickly rose.

Carnegie specifies that these principles don't work with everyone or in every situation. But they are more effective than arguments and pressure.

- Assume honest intentions in others.
- Make your ideas concrete and vivid.
- A challenge stimulates the desire to excel.

=== Leading without hurting
--- Correcting without humiliating
kw: praise first · indirect · your own mistakes
The fourth part of the book is for those who must lead, correct or change others. Carnegie offers several principles for doing so without arousing resentment.

**Begin with sincere praise.** It is easier to hear an unpleasant remark after hearing sincere recognition of your qualities. Carnegie compares this to the barber who lathers before he shaves.

**Call attention to mistakes indirectly.** Carnegie warns against using the word "but" after a compliment, which cancels the compliment. He suggests instead linking the two ideas with "and," or pointing out the mistake indirectly, through an example or a suggestion.

**Talk about your own mistakes before criticizing others'.** Carnegie recounts that he had been irritated by his young secretary's mistakes. Then he remembered his own blunders at the same age. So he began by mentioning them before suggesting improvements. The secretary accepted the remark without feeling humiliated.

**Ask questions instead of giving orders.** A question such as "Do you think this might work?" leaves the other person room to think and propose. It is more readily accepted than an order.

**Let the other person save face.** Even when you are right, humiliating the other person is a mistake. Leave them an honorable way out.

> À retenir | Start with recognition, point out indirectly, mention your own mistakes, ask rather than order, let people save face.

--- Encouraging progress
kw: every improvement · reputation · easy
Carnegie continues with principles for encouraging change.

**Praise the slightest improvement.** Animal trainers reward every small step forward. Carnegie suggests doing the same with people: notice and acknowledge every improvement, however modest. This encourages them to keep going. He cites examples of children and adults whose path changed because someone noticed and encouraged their first efforts.

**Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.** If you tell someone you know they are capable, conscientious or honest, they tend to act to deserve that image. Carnegie tells the story of a careless employee whose manager publicly highlighted his past competence. The employee returned to his former level.

**Use encouragement, and make the fault seem easy to correct.** If you present a problem as insurmountable, the person gets discouraged. If you present it as easy to fix, they gain confidence. Carnegie recounts how one dance teacher encouraged him by telling him he had a natural sense of rhythm, whereas another had discouraged him by listing all his faults.

**Make the other person happy about doing what you suggest.** Present a request so that the other person sees in it an advantage, recognition or a rewarding responsibility.

Carnegie stresses that these principles are not foolproof recipes, but they greatly increase the chances of lasting change.

- Acknowledge every small improvement.
- Give people a reputation to live up to.
- Present the correction as easy.

=== Putting it into practice
--- 30-day action plan
kw: week by week · one principle
**Week 1: stop criticizing.** For a week, avoid all criticism, complaints and reproaches. When the urge comes, try to understand the other person's reasons. Each evening, note the situations where you succeeded or failed.

**Week 2: appreciate and listen.** Express at least one sincere, specific appreciation every day. In your conversations, ask more questions and talk less about yourself. Make an effort to remember and use first names.

**Week 3: persuade without confrontation.** In a disagreement, start with the points of agreement. Replace "you're wrong" with cautious phrasing. If you were wrong, admit it quickly.

**Week 4: lead without hurting.** If you have to correct someone, start with sincere recognition, mention your own mistakes, ask questions rather than give orders. Notice and acknowledge every improvement.

Carnegie recommends rereading the principles regularly and reviewing their application each week.

> À retenir | One principle at a time, applied in real life, with a review every week.

--- Case study: the site manager
kw: tensions · subcontractors · deadlines
Mathieu is a site manager at a construction company. His relations with the subcontractor crews are tense: he often criticizes them in front of everyone, deadlines are slipping, and several tradespeople refuse to work with him again. His director advises him to read Carnegie.

**Stop criticizing.** Mathieu decides to stop reprimanding people publicly. When a job is poorly done, he takes the person aside and starts by asking what happened. He discovers that several delays come from material deliveries poorly organized by his own company.

**Take an interest in others.** He learns the tradespeople's first names, takes an interest in their constraints, and takes time to chat with them in the morning.

**Admit mistakes.** He acknowledges the delivery problems in front of the crews and commits to fixing them.

**Encourage.** When a crew completes a stage on time, he highlights it at the site meeting.

**Throw down a challenge.** Each week he posts the progress of the different zones, which creates healthy competition.

Three months later, the site has made up part of its delay, and several tradespeople are asking to work with him again.

> Question de réflexion | Which tense working relationship might change if you stopped criticizing for a month?

=== Critical view and connections
--- Limits of the book
kw: era · manipulation · anecdotes
*How to Win Friends and Influence People* is a classic, but it has several limits.

**A book of its time.** The examples come mainly from American business and politics of the 1920s and 1930s. Some passages reflect the social roles of that era.

**The risk of manipulation.** Although Carnegie insists on sincerity, his principles can be used in a calculated way to get something from others. The title itself, with the word "influence," has fed this reading.

**Anecdotal evidence.** The book rests on stories and testimonials, not rigorous studies. Several of its intuitions have been confirmed by social psychology, such as the importance of listening, recognition and reciprocity, but others remain unverified.

**A conflict-avoiding outlook.** Carnegie favors harmony and tact. But some situations call for direct candor, open disagreement or confrontation. More recent authors, such as Kim Scott, stress the importance of saying things clearly.

**An individual approach.** The book deals with relationships between people, not with problems of organization, power or injustice that also explain many conflicts.

- Sound intuitions, but anecdotal evidence.
- Tact must not prevent candor.

--- Connections with other books
kw: Cialdini · Covey · Scott · Voss
**With Influence (Cialdini).** Several of Carnegie's principles, such as appreciation, liking and gradual commitment, correspond to mechanisms studied scientifically by Cialdini.

**With The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People (Covey).** The habit "seek first to understand, then to be understood" directly extends Carnegie's emphasis on listening. Covey, however, criticizes approaches centered on technique rather than character.

**With Radical Candor (Scott).** Kim Scott complements Carnegie: you must care sincerely about others, but also dare to tell them clearly what is wrong.

**With Never Split the Difference (Voss).** Voss's tactical empathy, which consists of understanding and naming the other person's emotions, echoes Carnegie's idea of seeing things from the other person's point of view.

**With Crucial Conversations.** This book offers a more structured method for high-stakes discussions, where Carnegie mainly gives principles of attitude.

> Parcours conseillé | Read Crucial Conversations next for difficult discussions, then Influence to understand the mechanisms of persuasion.
