=== Context and thesis
--- A leader in the storm
kw: Loudcloud · Opsware · 2000
Ben Horowitz is an American entrepreneur and investor. In 1999, with Marc Andreessen, creator of the first mainstream web browser, he founded Loudcloud, a company that offered to host and manage the IT infrastructure of websites. The company quickly raised a lot of money and grew very fast.

A few months later, the internet bubble burst. Loudcloud's customers, often young web companies, went bankrupt one after another. Investors pulled out. Horowitz, who had become CEO, faced a series of crises: an IPO in a collapsed market, successive layoffs, the loss of major customers, the constant threat of bankruptcy.

To save the company, he made a radical decision. He sold the hosting business, which accounted for most of the revenue, and turned the company into a software vendor under the name Opsware. The transformation was painful, but it succeeded. In 2007, Opsware was acquired by Hewlett-Packard for about $1.6 billion.

In 2009, Horowitz and Andreessen founded a venture capital firm, Andreessen Horowitz, which would become one of the most influential in Silicon Valley. Horowitz began writing a blog in which he shared, without varnish, what he had learned as a leader. *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, published in 2014, collects and expands those texts.

The book does not look like the usual management books. It is personal, direct, sometimes brutal, and each chapter opens with a rap quote, the author's favorite music.

> À retenir | The account of a leader who lived through the collapse of his market and transformed his company to survive.

--- There is no recipe
kw: no solution · manuals · honesty
The book's main thesis is stated in its title: what is truly hard about being a leader is not covered by management books.

Horowitz explains that he read many management books, and that they give good advice for ordinary situations: setting goals, motivating teams, organizing work. But they say nothing about the situations that actually kept him up at night. What do you do when you have promised your employees the company will succeed and it is on the verge of bankruptcy? What do you do when a close friend who helped found the company is no longer up to the job? What do you do when your best engineers want to leave? How do you announce layoffs?

For Horowitz, these situations have no ready-made solution. Each is unique and involves choices between options that are all bad. There is no formula for resolving them. But it is possible to share the experience of those who have lived through them, to help other leaders face them.

He also stresses the importance of **honesty**. Many entrepreneurs' stories present their success as the result of a clear vision and good decisions. Horowitz, by contrast, tells of his doubts, his mistakes, his moments of despair. He wants to show leaders that their difficulties are normal and that they are not alone.

The book is aimed first at leaders of tech start-ups, but many of its lessons apply to any manager facing hard decisions.

- Manuals cover the easy situations.
- Hard situations have no recipe.
- Sharing experience is better than claiming a formula.

=== The struggle
--- The fight
kw: despair · loneliness · keep going
Horowitz devotes an entire chapter to what he calls **the struggle**. It is the state a leader is in when everything seems to be going wrong and there is no way out in sight.

He describes this state precisely. The leader wonders why he started the company. He feels that everyone thinks he is incompetent. He no longer sleeps. He has promised his employees, his investors and his family that everything would be fine, and he knows it isn't true. He can't share his doubts with his teams for fear of demoralizing them. He feels deeply alone.

Horowitz explains that the struggle is not failure. It is part of the path of almost every successful entrepreneur and leader. Biographies and articles about great companies often leave it out, which gives the impression that others never went through such moments. That is false.

He offers no miracle cure, but a few ways to get through the struggle.

**Don't carry the whole weight alone.** Share some difficulties with trusted people, within the leadership team, with a mentor or with other leaders.

**Remember that this is not checkers.** Situations evolve, markets change, new options appear. What seems hopeless today may change tomorrow.

**Don't give up too soon.** Many companies that eventually succeeded were on the verge of giving up at some point.

He insists on one point: greatness often comes from the ability to get through these moments rather than from their absence.

> À retenir | Doubt and loneliness are part of the path. The difference often lies in the ability to keep going.

--- No silver bullet
kw: magic solutions · patient work
Horowitz tells an episode from Opsware's history. After Loudcloud's transformation, the company faced a competitor whose product many customers considered better. The leadership team searched for solutions. Several ideas came up: change the sales strategy, reposition, target other customers, form partnerships.

Horowitz understood that these ideas were all attempts to avoid the real problem: the product was not good enough. He decided to focus all the company's energy on improving the product, to make it better than the competitor's. It was long, hard, unglamorous work that demanded months of effort. But it worked: a few months later, Opsware had regained the advantage.

He draws a lesson from it. Faced with a serious problem, leaders often look for a **magic solution**, a brilliant idea that would fix everything at once. Horowitz uses the image of the silver bullet, the weapon that, in legend, can kill a werewolf in a single shot. But in the reality of companies, such magic solutions almost never exist. Most often, you have to confront the problem directly, with a lot of patient work: what he calls lead bullets.

He stresses that the search for magic solutions is often a form of avoidance. It is more pleasant to imagine a new strategy than to admit that the product, or the team, or the organization has a fundamental flaw.

- Faced with a serious problem, there is generally no magic solution.
- Hard, patient work on the real problem is often the only way.
- The search for a brilliant idea can be a form of avoidance.

=== The painful decisions
--- Laying people off properly
kw: speed · the manager tells · respect
Horowitz had to carry out several waves of layoffs at Loudcloud. He draws very concrete lessons from them, which he considers among the most important in the book.

**Own it.** The leader must acknowledge that the layoffs are the consequence of his own mistakes or the company's: growth that was too fast, a strategy that didn't work, a market poorly anticipated. He must not present the decision as an external inevitability.

**Act fast.** Once the decision is made, it must be carried out quickly. If rumors spread before the announcement, the company is paralyzed and the best people start to leave.

**Be clear about your intentions.** The leader must explain to the whole company why the layoffs are necessary and what they are meant to achieve.

**Train the managers.** Each manager must be prepared to break the news to their own people.

**The manager tells them personally.** Horowitz insists on this point. It is not for HR or a consultant to tell people they are being laid off. It is for their manager, the one who hired or led them. People who are laid off will remember for the rest of their lives how they were treated. So will those who stay.

**Address the whole company.** After the individual announcements, the leader must speak to all employees, especially those who stay and wonder whether their turn will come.

**Be present.** The leader must be visible and available in the following days, to answer questions and show respect for those who are leaving.

> À retenir | Own it, act fast, train the managers, and have each manager deliver the news personally. How it is done matters as much as the decision.

--- Letting an executive go
kw: diagnosis · hiring · candor
Horowitz addresses an even more delicate situation: letting go of a member of the executive team.

He starts with an honest diagnosis. If a hired executive is not succeeding, the first question to ask is: **why did I hire this person?** Often, the problem lies in the hiring process: the CEO had not clearly defined what he expected of the role, he was seduced by a personality or a prestigious résumé, he did not check the skills actually needed. Or the company has changed, and the role is no longer the one the person was hired for.

He distinguishes several causes of failure. An executive may have been hired for the wrong company size: excellent in a large structured company, lost in a start-up, or the reverse. He may have personal ambition that comes before the company's interest. He may not know how to integrate into the existing team.

For the separation itself, Horowitz recommends acting with **candor and respect**. The CEO must explain the reasons for his decision clearly, without beating around the bush. He must also acknowledge his own share of responsibility for the hiring mistake. He advises preparing the terms of departure carefully, including how it will be announced to the company, to preserve the person's dignity.

He also tells the particular case of a close friend he had to demote. He explains that friendship makes the decision harder, but must not prevent it. Keeping someone in a role they cannot handle harms the company, the team and often the person themselves.

- First ask yourself why you hired this person.
- Be candid, acknowledge your part, preserve dignity.
- Friendship must not prevent the decision.

--- Telling it like it is
kw: bad news · trust
Horowitz insists on the importance of honesty toward employees, especially when the news is bad.

He recounts that in Loudcloud's hard times he was tempted to present the situation in a more favorable light than it really was, so as not to demoralize the teams. He understood that this was a mistake. Employees are not naive. They sense when something is wrong. If the leader hides the truth, they lose trust in him. And when the truth eventually comes out, trust is destroyed.

He explains that telling the truth also has a practical advantage. Problems shared with the whole company can be solved by more people. If the leader keeps bad news to himself, he faces the problems alone. If he shares them, dozens or hundreds of people can look for solutions.

He warns against a technique often taught to managers: sandwiching a criticism between two compliments. Horowitz believes that experienced people quickly see through this technique and end up no longer believing the compliments. He recommends instead being direct while being sincerely caring.

Finally, he points out that transparency has limits. Some information, such as ongoing negotiations or personal data, cannot be shared. But the principle should be to share as much as possible and to explain why certain information cannot be shared.

> Question clé | What bad news are you keeping to yourself, when your team could help solve it?

=== Taking care of people
--- A good place to work
kw: culture · process · results
Horowitz asserts that taking care of the people, the products and the profits, in that order, is a priority for a leader. He presents this idea not as a form of kindness but as a practical necessity.

He explains what he means by **a good place to work**. It is not a company with spectacular perks or luxurious offices. It is a company where people can focus on their work and know that if they do it well, they will be rewarded and the company will benefit. In a bad organization, people spend their time fighting internal obstacles, politics and absurd procedures, and their work doesn't count.

He stresses that when things are going well, the quality of the organization matters little: growth covers the flaws. But when difficulties arrive, as they always do, only a good organization keeps people. In a bad organization, the best leave as soon as things deteriorate.

He insists on the importance of **training**. Many start-ups see training as a luxury they can't afford. Horowitz thinks the opposite: training employees, especially managers, is one of the highest-return activities. He describes running training sessions for his managers himself, explaining precisely what he expected of them.

He also describes a document he wrote for product managers, contrasting a good product manager with a bad one. This kind of very concrete document clarifies expectations and helps train newcomers.

> À retenir | A good place to work is one where the work matters and people can devote themselves to it. It makes the difference in a crisis.

--- Training and clarifying expectations
kw: training · job descriptions · managers
Horowitz devotes several passages to training and developing managers.

He explains that many fast-growing companies promote people to manager without explaining what is expected of them. These new managers improvise, copy what they have seen, and make mistakes that demotivate their teams.

He recommends several practices.

**Train all newcomers.** An onboarding program lets everyone quickly understand the company, its products, its customers and its methods.

**Train managers specifically.** Managers must learn to hire, run one-on-ones, give feedback and handle conflict. Horowitz believes the CEO should personally take part in this training, to pass on his expectations.

**Clarify expectations in writing.** For each important role, describe what a good and a bad holder of the job looks like. These descriptions prevent misunderstandings and serve as a basis for feedback.

**Hold regular one-on-ones.** Horowitz considers that these meetings should be devoted to the employee's concerns, not the manager's.

He also highlights a risk for fast-growing companies: the **dilution** of culture and competence. When headcount doubles every year, most employees are new and have not yet learned the company's methods. Without training, quality deteriorates quickly.

- Train all newcomers, and above all the managers.
- Write down what a good and a bad holder of each role looks like.
- In rapid growth, training protects the culture.

--- Don't over-promote
kw: titles · politics · clear criteria
Horowitz addresses the often underestimated question of titles and promotions.

He observes that in growing companies, the temptation to hand out titles generously is strong. They cost nothing and help recruit or retain people. But he warns against several dangers.

**Title inflation.** If everyone becomes a director or vice president, titles lose their meaning. Competent people feel devalued when others, less effective, get the same titles.

**Politics.** When promotion criteria are vague, promotions become the object of political maneuvering. People spend their time promoting themselves to decision-makers rather than doing their work. Horowitz considers the leader responsible for politics in his organization: he creates it through poorly framed decisions.

**The Peter principle.** People are often promoted until they reach a position where they are no longer competent. An excellent engineer can become a poor manager.

To avoid these drifts, he recommends establishing **clear, known criteria** for each level, and a formal promotion process applied the same way to everyone. He also advises resisting promotion requests presented as conditions for staying with the company, because giving in encourages others to do the same.

He stresses that politics in an organization does not come from human nature but from the incentives created by leadership. A leader who rewards those who complain or apply pressure will get an organization where everyone complains and applies pressure.

> Question clé | Are your promotion criteria clear and known to all, or do they leave room for politics?

--- Keeping your best people
kw: departures · listening · no counteroffers
Horowitz addresses a situation every leader dreads: a key employee announces they want to leave.

He explains that the first reaction is often to immediately offer a raise, a promotion or a new title. Horowitz warns against this reaction. If the departure is driven by other reasons, such as disagreement with leadership, lack of interest in the work or a poor relationship with their manager, money will solve nothing. And if the company gives in to every threat of departure, it teaches all employees that threatening to leave is the best way to get a raise.

He recommends instead trying to **understand**. Why does the person want to leave? What is missing for them? Often, the underlying reasons are not the ones expressed first.

Above all, he stresses the importance of **prevention**. The departures of the best people are rarely sudden. They are preceded by signs: a drop in enthusiasm, frustrations voiced, requests left unanswered. Regular one-on-ones, in which the manager really listens, make it possible to spot these signs and act before it is too late.

He also says that he sometimes let go of people he could have kept, because he thought their departure was better for them or for the company. Not every departure is a failure.

Finally, he reminds us that the best way to keep the best people is to build a good place to work, where the work matters, people grow and they are treated fairly.

- Don't systematically counteroffer when someone threatens to leave.
- Understand the real reasons.
- Prevent through regular listening.

=== Leading in wartime
--- Peacetime, wartime
kw: existential threat · two styles
Horowitz proposes a distinction that has made a strong impression on readers: between the **peacetime** CEO and the **wartime** CEO.

A company is in peacetime when it has a significant advantage over its competitors and its market is growing. The leader can then focus on expanding the market, reinforcing strengths, creativity and improving the culture. He can tolerate a degree of dispersion, encourage everyone's initiative and take time to build consensus.

A company is in wartime when it faces an **existential threat**: a competitor overtaking it, an economic crisis, a technological shift, a cash shortage. The leader must then act very differently. He must focus the company on a single objective, impose strict discipline, make fast decisions, sometimes alone, and not hesitate to shake up habits.

Horowitz gives a few contrasts to illustrate the difference. The peacetime CEO knows that following rules and processes promotes success; the wartime CEO knows that sometimes the rules must be broken to survive. The first seeks consensus; the second has little tolerance for disagreements that slow action. The first builds broad, diverse teams; the second concentrates resources on the mission.

He stresses that both styles are legitimate and that few leaders excel at both. The hardest part is knowing which situation you are in, and changing style when the situation changes.

> À retenir | In peacetime, expand and grow. In wartime, focus and decide fast. The trap is misreading the situation.

--- The Opsware transformation
kw: sell the business · pivot · survival
Horowitz recounts in detail the decision that saved his company, an example of wartime leadership.

In 2002, Loudcloud was publicly listed, but its hosting business was losing money, its customers were disappearing and its cash was running out. Investors had lost confidence. Horowitz and his team studied every option and reached a hard conclusion: the company's core business had no future in the context of the time.

On the other hand, Loudcloud had developed, for its own needs, software that automated server management. This software could be sold to other companies. Horowitz then proposed selling the hosting business to a large IT services provider, EDS, and turning the company into a software vendor.

The decision was very risky. It meant giving up most of the revenue and a large share of the employees, who would leave with the business being sold. Investors had to approve it, and the market had to believe in the new project. Horowitz describes the tense negotiations, the moments when all seemed lost, and the relief when the deal was closed.

After the sale, the new company, Opsware, still went through hard years: a very low share price, powerful competitors, hesitant customers. But it gradually established itself, until its acquisition by Hewlett-Packard in 2007.

Horowitz takes from this episode that sometimes you have to give up what made up the company's identity to save what can be saved.

- Sometimes survival requires giving up your core business.
- The decision must be made before it is too late.

--- Sell or carry on
kw: acquisition · independence · decision
Horowitz recounts another hard decision: should you sell the company?

In 2007, Opsware was back on solid ground. Its product was recognized and its sales were growing. Several large companies were interested in it. Hewlett-Packard made a substantial offer. Horowitz had to decide whether to accept it, or carry on alone in the hope of building a much larger company.

He describes the arguments on both sides. Staying independent would make it possible to pursue the company's vision, keep its culture and perhaps create more value in the long run. Selling would guarantee a significant outcome for employees and shareholders, and protect the company against the risks of a changing market in which very large competitors were growing stronger.

He suggests a way of thinking about the question. The leader must ask whether his market is still at the start of its growth, in which case a well-positioned company can become dominant, or whether it is consolidating around a few big players. He must also ask whether his company has the capacity to become one of those players.

Horowitz explains that he concluded Opsware had little chance of becoming an independent leader against the IT giants, and that selling was the best decision for its employees and shareholders. He admits the decision was emotionally very hard: he had devoted years of his life to the company.

He stresses that, as with other hard decisions, there is no universal right answer. What matters is asking the right questions, honestly, without letting personal attachment take over.

> Question clé | In the decisions you have to make, how much comes from analysis, and how much from your personal attachment?

=== The leader's psychology
--- The hardest skill
kw: emotions · fear · not being crushed
Horowitz asserts that the hardest skill for a CEO is not strategy, financial management or hiring, but **managing his own psychology**.

He explains that the leader is under constant emotional pressure. He bears responsibility for his employees' jobs, his investors' money and his customers' trust. He must make hard decisions with incomplete information. He receives more bad news than good, because it is problems that make their way up to him.

This pressure can produce two dangerous reactions. The first is **taking everything too personally**: every problem becomes a catastrophe, the leader wears himself out and makes panicked decisions. The second is **not taking things seriously enough**: to protect himself, the leader minimizes problems, refuses to see reality and fails to react in time.

Horowitz offers a few ways to manage this pressure better.

**Make friends with other leaders**, who understand what you are going through and can share their experience.

**Write down your thoughts.** Putting the reasons for a hard decision in writing helps clarify your thinking and gain perspective.

**Focus on the road, not the wall.** He compares the leader to a race car driver: when you look at the wall, you end up hitting it. Focus on what you can do, not on what you fear.

He notes that successful leaders are not those who feel no fear, but those who act despite it.

> À retenir | The hardest skill is keeping a clear head under pressure. Look at the road, not the wall.

--- Courage and fear
kw: hero · coward · decisions
Horowitz takes up an idea he attributes to a famous boxing trainer: the hero and the coward feel exactly the same fear. What sets them apart is what they do with it.

He applies this idea to leaders. Faced with a hard decision, laying people off, killing a product, standing up to an investor, every leader is afraid. The coward avoids the decision, postpones it, looks for a compromise that solves nothing. The hero makes the decision despite his fear.

He stresses that courage shows not only in big decisions, but in a multitude of small daily ones. Each time a leader chooses the easy way over the right decision, he loses a little courage, and it becomes harder to make the right choice the next time. Conversely, each courageous decision strengthens the ability to make others.

Horowitz notes that the hardest decisions are often those where **everyone disagrees** with the leader: employees, investors, experts. In those moments, he must rely on his own analysis and accept being alone. He says several of his best decisions were made against the opinion of the majority.

He adds a caveat, however: courage is not stubbornness. The leader must listen to opposing views, understand them, and change his mind when the arguments are good. Courage means deciding after listening, not ignoring others.

- Courage is not the absence of fear, but action despite it.
- It is built in small daily decisions.
- Listen, then decide, even alone.

--- Learning to be a CEO
kw: founder · learning · mistakes
Horowitz reflects on a question often debated in Silicon Valley: should founders stay at the head of their company, or make way for experienced executives?

He explains that for a long time investors often replaced founders with professional executives as the company grew. The reasoning was that founders are good inventors but poor managers.

Horowitz defends another position. In his view, founders often have a decisive advantage: they know the product, the technology and the company's vision deeply. They have the moral authority to make hard decisions, such as completely changing direction. An outside executive, however competent, finds this harder.

But he acknowledges that founders must **learn** the job of CEO, and that this learning is hard. They must acquire skills they don't have: hiring executives, managing an organization, understanding finance, communicating with investors. They inevitably make mistakes.

He suggests a way of looking at this learning. The leader must accept being bad at some things at first, and actively seek to improve: by asking for feedback, observing other leaders, reading, getting coaching. He compares this learning to a sport: you don't get good without practicing and making mistakes.

He also stresses the importance of distinguishing decisions the leader must make himself from those he can delegate. A founder who wants to control everything becomes his company's bottleneck.

- Founders have an advantage: deep knowledge and moral authority.
- They must learn the job of CEO, accepting being bad at first.
- Know what to keep and what to delegate.

=== Hiring and organizing
--- Hiring executives
kw: define the need · strengths · onboarding
Horowitz devotes several chapters to hiring executives, which he considers one of founders' greatest challenges.

He explains that a founder hiring a head of sales, a CFO or a CTO for the first time is often poorly equipped to do so. He doesn't know the job in question well, he doesn't know how to evaluate candidates, and he risks being seduced by prestigious résumés.

He proposes an approach.

**Know what you want.** Before hiring, the leader must define precisely what he expects from the role. If necessary, he must learn the job himself, by talking to experts, to understand what an excellent holder looks like.

**Look for strengths, not the absence of weaknesses.** A candidate with no apparent flaws is not necessarily the best. It is better to look for a candidate who is exceptional in the skills that matter most for the role, even if they have weaknesses elsewhere.

**Hire for the company's current and future size.** An executive from a very large company may be at a loss in a small organization without resources. A start-up executive may not know how to run a large organization.

**Take care with onboarding.** An executive hired from outside must quickly understand the company's culture, methods and relationships. Horowitz recommends setting clear objectives from the start and following their early days closely.

He also addresses **internal promotion**. Promoting a loyal employee has advantages, since they know the company and the culture, but you must check they have the skills for the new role.

> À retenir | Know precisely what you are looking for, favor exceptional strengths, and support onboarding.

--- Organizing the company
kw: communication · trade-offs · structure
Horowitz also gives advice on organizing the company, which he approaches pragmatically.

He explains that every organizational structure is a **trade-off**. It makes communication easier between some people, those on the same team or under the same manager, and harder between others. There is no perfect structure. You must choose the communication paths that matter most to the company at a given moment and organize teams accordingly.

He proposes an approach: identify the most critical flows of information and decisions, group the people who need to communicate most often, appoint a leader for each group, and put mechanisms in place for communication between groups.

He recommends **accepting the imperfections** of the chosen structure and compensating for them with processes: coordination meetings, shared tools, people in charge of links between teams.

He stresses that reorganizations are always costly. They disrupt relationships, create uncertainty and can cause departures. He advises not multiplying them and preparing them carefully.

He also insists on the importance of **processes** as the company grows. A small team can run on informal exchanges. Once it passes a few dozen people, some practices must be formalized, without falling into bureaucracy.

- Every structure makes some communication easier and other communication harder.
- Choose based on the most critical flows, compensate with processes.
- Don't multiply reorganizations.

--- One-on-ones that get people talking
kw: questions · listening · weak signals
Horowitz insists on the importance of regular one-on-ones between manager and employee. He sees them as one of the main ways to bring information up and to spot problems before they become serious.

He specifies that these meetings should be organized around the employee's concerns. The manager must above all listen. To start the conversation, Horowitz suggests open questions, in this spirit:

- If we could improve one thing about the way we work, what would it be?
- What is the main problem in our organization, in your view, and why?
- What is not enjoyable about your job right now?
- Who in the company is doing remarkable work?
- If you were in my shoes, what would you change?
- What could make us lose to our competitors?

Horowitz explains that these questions serve several purposes. They show the employee that their opinion matters. They let the manager discover problems he can't see from his position. They bring weak signals to the surface: a budding frustration, a conflict, an untapped idea.

He stresses that the quality of these meetings depends on trust. If the employee fears their criticism will be held against them, they will say nothing important. The manager must therefore thank people for criticism, show that he takes it into account, and never use it against the person.

> Exercice | In your next one-on-one, ask a single open question, for example: "If you were in my shoes, what would you change?" Listen without defending yourself.

=== Putting it into practice
--- 30-day action plan
kw: week by week · truth · decisions
**Week 1: face reality.** Write one page on the real situation of your team or business, without embellishing it. Identify the most serious problem, and ask yourself whether you are looking for a magic solution to avoid confronting it. Determine whether you are in peacetime or wartime.

**Week 2: share.** Share a piece of bad news with your team, honestly, and ask for their help in solving it. Identify one or two people outside your team with whom you can talk about your difficulties.

**Week 3: the postponed decision.** List the hard decisions you are putting off. Choose one. Write down the reasons to make it and not to make it. Decide, and carefully prepare how you will announce it.

**Week 4: people.** For a key role on your team, write what an excellent holder and a poor holder look like. Share this document. Check that your promotion or evaluation criteria are clear to everyone.

Horowitz reminds us that no plan makes these decisions easy. But facing them is better than avoiding them.

> À retenir | Face reality, share it, make the postponed decision, clarify expectations. Without looking for a silver bullet.

--- Case study: the agency in crisis
kw: losing a client · layoffs · pivot
A forty-person communications agency earns half its revenue from a single client, a large retail group. One morning, this client announces it is changing agencies. The director, Claire, finds herself in wartime.

**Reality.** Claire redoes her numbers: without this client, the agency cannot pay salaries beyond four months. She is tempted to look for a magic solution, such as a big prospecting campaign, or to hide the situation so as not to alarm the teams.

**The truth.** She gathers the whole agency and explains the situation without embellishing it. Several employees propose ideas, and some agree to temporarily reduce their working hours.

**The real problem.** With her leadership team, she realizes that dependence on a single client was the real problem, and that the agency had no clear positioning to attract others. She decides to focus on a sector where the agency has recognized expertise.

**The painful decision.** Despite the efforts, she has to let eight people go. Each team leader personally breaks the news to their people, after being prepared. Claire then addresses everyone and remains available.

**What happened next.** A year later, the agency has thirty-five people, and no client accounts for more than a fifth of its business.

> Question de réflexion | What dependency in your business could tip you into wartime overnight?

=== Critical view and connections
--- Limits of the book
kw: start-ups · tone · survivor
*The Hard Thing About Hard Things* is valued for its candor, but it has several limits.

**A very particular context.** The book is written for leaders of Silicon Valley tech start-ups, with their fundraising, explosive growth and brutal crises. Much of the advice is less relevant for a family business, a public administration or a stable organization.

**Survivorship bias.** Horowitz tells a story that ended well. Leaders who made similar decisions failed. It is hard to know how much of Opsware's success was due to the author's decisions and how much to luck.

**A sometimes brutal tone.** The direct style, the references to rap and war, and the celebration of the lone leader who decides against everyone may put some readers off. The war metaphor can also justify authoritarian behavior.

**A loosely structured book.** The chapters are reorganized blog posts. Readers find valuable lessons, but not a coherent method.

**The place of others.** The book centers on the CEO. It says little about how employees experience these crises, or about how decisions could be made more collectively.

- A valuable account, but rooted in the start-up world.
- A success story told by the person who lived it.

--- Connections with other books
kw: Grove · Christensen · Marquet · Scott
**With High Output Management (Grove).** Horowitz often quotes Andy Grove, whom he admires. Grove describes management under normal conditions; Horowitz adds crisis situations. Grove himself wrote about the "strategic inflection points" that threaten a company's existence.

**With The Innovator's Dilemma (Christensen).** Loudcloud's transformation into Opsware illustrates how hard it is to give up your core business in the face of a market shift.

**With Turn the Ship Around! (Marquet).** The two books describe very different leadership styles. Marquet decentralizes decisions; Horowitz defends, in wartime, a leader who makes the call. The two can complement each other depending on the situation.

**With Radical Candor (Scott).** Horowitz and Kim Scott share the conviction that you must tell people the truth, directly but with sincere care for them.

**With The Making of a Manager (Zhuo).** Horowitz's advice on one-on-ones, devoted to the employee's concerns, echoes Julie Zhuo's.

> Parcours conseillé | Read High Output Management next for the fundamentals, then The Innovator's Dilemma to understand market disruptions.
