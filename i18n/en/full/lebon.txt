=== Context and thesis
--- An author and his era
kw: nineteenth century · revolutions · era of crowds
Gustave Le Bon was a French physician, anthropologist and essayist, born in 1841. He took an interest in many fields: medicine, archaeology, photography, physics. He is best known for *The Crowd* (French original: *Psychologie des foules*), published in 1895.

The book appeared in a France marked by a turbulent political history. The century had seen several revolutions, the Paris Commune in 1871, the rise of workers' movements, the spread of universal suffrage and the mass press. The elites of the time were worried about the growing place of the popular masses in political life.

Le Bon argues that we are entering the **era of crowds**. In his view, the great religious and political beliefs that organized societies are collapsing, and power is gradually passing to the masses. He wants to understand the psychology of these crowds, so that leaders can understand them and, if need be, govern them.

The book must be read with its context in mind. Le Bon expresses a very distrustful, often contemptuous view of the working classes, and he defends ideas about "races" and about women that are rejected today. His book was also read with interest by twentieth-century authoritarian leaders, who found in it techniques for manipulating the masses.

Yet the work remains a classic, often cited in social psychology, political science and communication. Some of its observations about collective behavior remain relevant, even though its explanations have been widely challenged.

> À retenir | An 1895 classic to be read critically: striking observations about crowds, mixed with the prejudices of its time.

--- The law of mental unity
kw: collective mind · transformation
Le Bon's central thesis is that the individual is **transformed** on becoming part of a crowd.

He first clarifies what he means by a crowd. It isn't merely a gathering of people in the same place. A crowd, in the psychological sense, is a set of people whose feelings and ideas point in the same direction and who form a kind of **collective mind**. A crowd can form in a street, in a parliamentary assembly, in a jury or in an entire nation at certain moments.

Le Bon formulates what he calls the **law of the mental unity of crowds**. When individuals form a crowd, their conscious personality fades. They think, feel and act very differently from how they would act alone. Differences in intelligence, education and character blur. What emerges are shared, more elementary feelings.

He highlights a striking consequence: a crowd is not the sum of the intelligences that make it up. In his view, it is often intellectually inferior to the isolated individual. Educated, reasonable people can, within a crowd, make absurd decisions or commit acts they would condemn on their own.

Le Bon adds, however, that the crowd isn't necessarily bad. It can be capable of acts of heroism and devotion that the isolated individual would not perform. It is **more extreme**, for good as for ill.

- The individual is transformed in the crowd.
- A collective mind erases individual differences.
- The crowd is more extreme, at its worst and at its best.

=== Why the individual changes
--- Anonymity, contagion, suggestion
kw: invincibility · imitation · hypnosis
Le Bon offers three explanations for the individual's transformation in the crowd.

**The sense of power and anonymity.** In a crowd, the individual feels carried by numbers. He acquires a sense of power that allows him to yield to instincts he would have restrained alone. And since he is anonymous, lost in the mass, he feels less responsible for his actions. Le Bon believes that individual responsibility, which usually restrains behavior, largely disappears.

**Contagion.** Feelings and acts spread through a crowd from person to person, like a disease. An individual adopts the behavior of others, to the point of sacrificing personal interest to the collective interest. Le Bon compares this contagion to a phenomenon of hypnosis.

**Suggestibility.** In his view, this is the most important cause. The individual in a crowd is in a state close to hypnosis, losing will and discernment and following whatever suggestions are given. Here Le Bon draws on the research of his time on hypnosis, very fashionable at the end of the nineteenth century.

Le Bon insists that these mechanisms act on all individuals, whatever their education. He cites examples of learned or political assemblies that, in his view, behaved like crowds.

Modern psychology has largely rejected the hypnosis explanation. But the phenomena of emotional contagion, diffusion of responsibility and social influence he describes were later studied, with other explanations.

> Question clé | Have you ever acted, in a group, in a way you would have disapproved of had you been alone?

--- The characteristics of crowds
kw: impulsive · credulous · intolerant
Le Bon describes several characteristics of crowds.

**Impulsiveness and changeability.** The crowd is guided by the unconscious and by emotion rather than reflection. It moves quickly from one feeling to another: from enthusiasm to anger, from adoration to hatred.

**Credulity.** The crowd readily accepts what it is told, without critical thought. It can believe the most implausible rumors. Le Bon mentions cases of collective hallucination, in which entire groups believe they see something that doesn't exist.

**Exaggeration and simplicity of feeling.** The crowd knows no nuance. It simplifies, exaggerates and pushes its feelings to extremes. A suspicion becomes a certainty; a dislike becomes hatred.

**Intolerance and authoritarianism.** The crowd tolerates contradiction poorly. It respects strength and despises weakness. It is ready to submit to an authoritarian leader.

**Conservatism.** Paradoxically, despite its apparently revolutionary character, the crowd is attached to deep traditions. It can overthrow a government, but quickly returns to familiar forms of power.

**Morality.** The crowd can be capable of cruelty, but also of self-denial, devotion and sacrifice for a cause.

Le Bon stresses that these characteristics vary with the type of crowd but are found to varying degrees in all human gatherings.

- Impulsive, credulous, simplistic, intolerant.
- Revolutionary on the surface, conservative underneath.
- Capable of cruelty as well as devotion.

=== The ideas and beliefs of crowds
--- Images, not reasons
kw: images · words · formulas
Le Bon explains that crowds are not moved by **reasoning**. They are influenced by **images**.

In his view, the crowd thinks in images. One image evokes another, with no logical link. What strikes a crowd's imagination is not an argument but a vivid, arresting, often simplified representation. He cites great events that mark public opinion less by their real importance than by their spectacular character. A disaster that kills many people in a single instant strikes minds more than a slow epidemic that kills far more.

He stresses the power of **words** and **formulas**. Certain words, such as liberty, equality, democracy, have considerable power over crowds, not because of their precise meaning, which the crowd doesn't know, but because of the images and emotions they evoke. The meaning of these words changes across eras and peoples. Le Bon observes that skillful leaders know how to rename things: replacing a word that has become unpopular with a more appealing one, without changing the reality.

He also discusses the role of **illusions**. Crowds, in his view, need illusions, dreams and beliefs that give meaning to their existence. Those who know how to supply them win their support. Those who try to destroy them become their enemies.

Finally, he stresses the importance of **experience**: only repeated, often painful experience can destroy an illusion firmly lodged in the minds of crowds.

> À retenir | Crowds think in images, not arguments. Words matter less for their meaning than for the emotions they evoke.

--- Beliefs and opinions
kw: fundamental · changeable · tradition
Le Bon distinguishes two types of conviction.

**Fundamental beliefs** are deep and lasting. They are the great religious, political or social beliefs that organize a civilization for centuries. They change very slowly, and their change brings about great historical transformations. Le Bon thinks that the period in which he is writing is one of transition, in which old beliefs are collapsing before new ones have been established.

**Changeable opinions** are superficial and shifting. They arise and disappear quickly, with fashions, events and the influence of the press. Le Bon observes that the opinions of his time have become increasingly unstable, owing to the weakening of great beliefs and the proliferation of newspapers.

He also describes the **remote factors** that shape crowds' beliefs: race, in the questionable sense Le Bon gives it, traditions, time, institutions and education. He attaches great importance to traditions, which he regards as a people's collective memory. He also thinks institutions cannot profoundly transform a people: they are the product of its character rather than its cause.

He criticizes the education of his time, which he considers too reliant on memorization and recitation and poorly suited to forming judgment.

These analyses mix interesting observations, on the slowness of cultural change or the instability of opinion, with theories rejected today, notably on race.

- Fundamental beliefs change slowly.
- Superficial opinions change quickly.
- Le Bon mixes insights with theories rejected today.

=== Leaders
--- The need for a leader
kw: will · faith · domination
Le Bon devotes a large part of his book to **leaders**. In his view, every crowd needs a leader. As soon as a certain number of people gather, they instinctively place themselves under the authority of one of them.

He describes the leader as a person with a **strong will** and intense **faith** in an idea. Le Bon notes that leaders are generally not thinkers. They are men of action, sometimes not very intelligent, but convinced. Their conviction is contagious. He believes that a leader's faith, even in a false idea, has more power over crowds than a scholar's reason.

He distinguishes two types of leaders. The first have an energetic but fleeting will: they can sweep a crowd into a one-off action. The second have a lasting will: they are the great founders of religions or empires, who can leave their mark on history for centuries.

Le Bon stresses that the crowd seeks **obedience** more than freedom. It is ready to follow a leader who gives it direction, certainties and a sense of strength. This observation, presented as a general truth, also reflects the author's distrust of democracy.

He notes that leaders' authority is often fragile. It depends on their success. A leader who fails can be abandoned, even attacked, by the crowd that adored him the day before.

> Question clé | In your organization, what persuades most: the quality of an argument, or the conviction of the person making it?

--- Affirmation, repetition, contagion
kw: affirm · repeat · spread
Le Bon describes three methods leaders use to instill an idea in the minds of crowds.

**Affirmation.** A pure and simple affirmation, without reasoning or proof, is one of the surest ways to get an idea into a crowd's mind. The more concise and unqualified the affirmation, the greater its effect. Le Bon notes that political and religious speeches often proceed this way.

**Repetition.** An affirmation only has real effect if it is repeated constantly, and as far as possible in the same terms. Repetition eventually lodges the idea in the unconscious, to the point that people forget who said it and come to believe it. Le Bon cites advertising, which tirelessly repeats the same messages, and whose effectiveness he already observed in his day.

**Contagion.** Once an idea has been sufficiently affirmed and repeated, it spreads on its own, by contagion, from person to person. Opinions and beliefs thus spread far more through imitation than through reasoning. Le Bon observes that fashions in clothing, the arts or ideas spread in the same way.

These three methods were widely studied and used in the twentieth century, in advertising and political propaganda. Modern psychological research has confirmed that a repeated statement seems truer, a phenomenon sometimes called the illusory truth effect.

- Affirm without demonstrating.
- Repeat in the same terms.
- Let the idea spread through imitation.

--- Prestige
kw: acquired · personal · fragile
Le Bon considers **prestige** the main source of the authority of leaders and ideas.

He defines it as a kind of domination that a person, a work or an idea exerts over our minds. Prestige paralyzes critical sense and arouses admiration, almost fascination.

He distinguishes two forms.

**Acquired prestige** comes from name, fortune, reputation or office. An officer in uniform, a judge, a noble title or an old institution possess it independently of the personal qualities of those who hold them.

**Personal prestige** is a quality peculiar to certain people, who exert a fascination over those around them even without title or office. Le Bon cites the great conquerors and founders of religions.

He stresses that prestige depends heavily on **success**. A failure can destroy it very quickly. And once it is questioned, it ceases to be prestige: it vanishes as soon as one begins to examine it critically.

This analysis connects with later work on authority and charisma, notably sociologist Max Weber's work on charismatic authority and social psychology's work on the influence of symbols of authority.

> À retenir | Prestige paralyzes judgment. It comes from status or from the person, and disappears as soon as it is questioned or fails.

=== Different kinds of crowds
--- Juries, voters, assemblies
kw: organized crowds · voters · parliaments
In the last part of his book, Le Bon applies his analyses to different types of crowds.

**Criminal crowds.** He examines episodes of collective violence during the French Revolution. He believes the participants were not necessarily criminals in the ordinary sense, but people transformed by the crowd, convinced they were doing their duty.

**Juries.** Le Bon observes that criminal juries, made up of ordinary citizens, often react more to emotions than to legal arguments. They are sensitive to the lawyer's prestige, the defendant's appearance and the images evoked.

**Voters.** He regards the electorate as a crowd, sensitive to affirmations, promises and candidates' prestige. He describes, cynically, what a candidate must do to win people over: affirm, promise, flatter and attack opponents. Despite this critical view, he acknowledges that he sees no better system than universal suffrage and believes it must be accepted.

**Parliamentary assemblies.** He observes that parliaments, despite the quality of their members, often show the characteristics of crowds: susceptibility to orators, simplification of ideas, sudden reversals, the influence of a few leaders.

Le Bon concludes that the societies of his time are increasingly governed by crowd psychology and that leaders must understand it in order to govern.

- Jurors, voters and members of parliament sometimes behave as crowds.
- Le Bon criticizes these institutions without proposing to replace them.

=== An ambiguous legacy
--- Influence and uses
kw: propaganda · advertising · social psychology
*The Crowd* has had considerable, and often troubling, influence.

The book was read by many political leaders in the twentieth century. Several dictators and propagandists admitted drawing on it for their techniques of mass manipulation: simple affirmation, repetition, the use of images and symbols, the cult of the leader. This influence contributed greatly to the book's sulfurous reputation.

It also influenced **advertising** and **public relations**. The pioneers of these fields in the early twentieth century studied crowd psychology to understand how to influence public opinion and consumer behavior.

In the **social sciences**, the book opened a field of research on collective behavior. Sigmund Freud wrote an essay on group psychology that discusses Le Bon's theses at length. But later researchers widely criticized his method, which rests on impressions and anecdotes rather than rigorous observation.

**Modern social psychology** has developed other explanations of group behavior. It has shown in particular that crowds are not necessarily irrational, that people in them often retain their judgment, and that collective behavior is partly explained by a shared social identity and group norms rather than a loss of personality.

> Question clé | Where do you recognize the techniques Le Bon describes today: in politics, advertising, social media?

--- Crowds in the digital age
kw: social media · virality · bubbles
More than a century after its publication, Le Bon's book is often reread in light of **social media** and digital communication.

Several phenomena he described seem to find echoes in online dynamics.

**Emotional contagion.** Content that provokes strong emotions, anger, outrage, enthusiasm, spreads much faster than nuanced content.

**Simplification.** Short formats favor simple affirmations, slogans and striking images.

**Repetition.** The same messages, repeated by many accounts, can end up seeming true.

**Anonymity.** Relative anonymity online can encourage behavior people would not adopt face to face.

**Leaders.** Personalities with large followings, sometimes endowed with strong personal prestige, sway entire communities.

But caution is needed. Digital crowds are not physical crowds. Researchers stress that online dynamics also depend on algorithms, platforms' business models and the deliberate strategies of certain actors. And many online collective movements have also enabled useful, thoughtful mobilization.

Le Bon's book can help identify certain mechanisms, but not fully explain them.

- Contagion, simplification and repetition are found online.
- But algorithms and business models also play a major role.

=== Putting it into practice
--- What managers can take from it
kw: meetings · communication · critical thinking
Despite its limits, Le Bon's book can shed light on certain aspects of organizational life.

**Meetings.** A group gathered together can make decisions none of its members would have made alone. Contagion, conformity and group pressure exist in committees and meetings. A good facilitator makes sure to gather individual views before collective discussion homogenizes them, for example by asking everyone to write down their opinion before discussing it.

**Communication.** Simple messages, repeated and illustrated with concrete images, have more impact than long arguments. A leader who wants a strategy understood must summarize it, repeat it and illustrate it. But this must be done honestly, without slipping into manipulation.

**Prestige.** In organizations, ideas are often judged by the status of the person proposing them rather than by their merit. An attentive manager makes sure good ideas can come from anywhere.

**Critical thinking.** Knowing the mechanisms Le Bon describes helps protect against them: be wary of statements repeated without proof, of collective emotions and of unquestioned authority.

> Exercice | At your next decision meeting, ask everyone to write down their view before any discussion. Compare with the final decision.

--- 30-day action plan
kw: week by week · critical eye
**Week 1: observe.** Each day, spot an example of affirmation, repetition or contagion in the media, advertising or social media. Note what feeling it is trying to provoke.

**Week 2: groups.** Observe the meetings you attend. Note the moments when the group follows a view without really discussing it, or when a collective emotion overrides analysis.

**Week 3: protect judgment.** Introduce practices that preserve individual views: written opinions before discussion, appointing someone to play devil's advocate, time for reflection before an important decision.

**Week 4: communicate honestly.** For an important message, summarize it in one sentence, illustrate it with a concrete image and repeat it, while giving access to the reasons and the data.

> À retenir | Spot the mechanisms, protect individual judgment, communicate simply but honestly.

=== Critical view and connections
--- Limits of the book
kw: prejudice · method · elitism
*The Crowd* must be read critically.

**Serious prejudices.** The book contains racialist theories and contemptuous remarks about women, non-European peoples and the working classes, which were widespread in certain circles of its time and are rejected today.

**An impressionistic method.** Le Bon proceeds by general assertions and selected examples, without rigorous investigation. Ironically, he often uses the very methods he describes: affirmation, repetition, striking images.

**An elitist, anti-democratic outlook.** The book expresses deep distrust of mass participation in political life. It presents the crowd as naturally irrational and inferior, which later research has largely qualified.

**Outdated explanations.** The explanation by hypnosis and suggestion no longer matches current knowledge. Social psychology has shown that collective behavior follows more complex, and often more rational, logics.

**Dangerous use.** The book served as a manual for propagandists and authoritarian regimes.

It nonetheless remains an important document for understanding the history of ideas, and some of its observations on emotional contagion and the power of repetition have been confirmed by later research.

- A text marked by prejudices rejected today.
- Insights, but a weak method and outdated explanations.

--- Connections with other books
kw: Cialdini · Kahneman · Harari · Morel
**With Influence (Cialdini).** Cialdini studies scientifically several mechanisms Le Bon described intuitively, such as social proof and authority.

**With Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman).** Susceptibility to images, emotions and repeated statements corresponds to the fast, intuitive workings of System 1.

**With Sapiens (Harari).** Harari shows that shared beliefs make large-scale cooperation possible; Le Bon mainly described their dangers.

**With Absurd Decisions (Morel).** Morel studies how competent groups collectively make absurd decisions, with a far more rigorous approach.

**With Team of Teams (McChrystal).** In contrast to Le Bon's view, McChrystal shows how a large collective can become smarter than its leaders through information sharing and decentralization.

> Parcours conseillé | Read Influence next for a scientific approach to persuasion, then Absurd Decisions on collective errors.
