=== Context and paradigms
--- Covey and the character ethic
kw: personality · character · principles
Stephen Covey (1932-2012) was an American professor and consultant. Trained in management at Harvard, he taught organizational behaviour at Brigham Young University in Utah before founding a leadership training company. *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, published in 1989, has sold tens of millions of copies worldwide and remains one of the most widely read personal development books.

Covey says his book grew out of a personal search. While preparing a thesis, he studied the success literature published in the United States since the late eighteenth century. He observed a profound shift. For about a hundred and fifty years, this literature rested on what he calls the **character ethic**: success was linked to qualities such as integrity, humility, fidelity, courage, justice, patience and hard work. After the First World War, it gradually turned to the **personality ethic**: success became a matter of image, attitudes, communication techniques and charm.

Covey does not reject techniques. He acknowledges that knowing how to communicate, having a positive attitude or managing your image can be useful. But he argues that these techniques are secondary. Used without solid character, they become manipulation and eventually backfire on the person who uses them. You can fool others for a while, but not for long, especially in close relationships.

He compares the situation to a farm. You cannot cheat nature: you have to sow, water, weed and wait before you harvest. In the same way, relationships of trust, competence and lasting effectiveness are built slowly, by applying principles. There is no shortcut.

The book therefore offers an "inside-out" approach. To change your situation, you must first change yourself: your paradigms, your character, your habits. The outward results come afterwards.

> À retenir | Techniques without character become manipulation. Lasting effectiveness is built from the inside out, like a harvest that can't be faked.

--- Paradigms
kw: lenses · shift in perspective · map
Covey introduces a central notion: the **paradigm**. A paradigm is the way we see the world, not in the visual sense but in the sense of perceiving, understanding and interpreting. It is like a map: it is not the territory, but it guides our movements. If the map is wrong, our efforts are useless, however good our intentions.

Covey tells a personal story that has become famous. One Sunday morning on the New York subway, he sees a man get on with his children. The children run around, shout and disturb all the passengers. The father, seated, does nothing. Covey eventually tells him, with some irritation, that his children are disturbing a lot of people and that perhaps he could calm them down. The man looks up, as if becoming aware of the situation, and replies that they have just come from the hospital, where their mother died an hour ago, that he doesn't know what to do, and that they probably don't know how to handle it either.

Covey says his view changed instantly. His irritation vanished, replaced by compassion. He did not have to force himself to change his attitude: it was his way of seeing the situation that changed, and his behaviour followed naturally.

This story illustrates an essential point of the book: our behaviours flow from our paradigms. If we want to change lastingly, it is not enough to work on our behaviours or attitudes. We must work on our paradigms, that is, on the way we see situations, other people and ourselves.

Covey also stresses that we are rarely aware of our paradigms. We think we see the world as it is, when in fact we see it as we are. Two people can observe the same situation and describe it very differently, each convinced they are right. Becoming aware of your own paradigms is therefore an important step toward effectiveness.

- Our behaviours flow from the way we see.
- Shifting a paradigm changes behaviour without willpower.
- We see the world as we are, not as it is.

--- The maturity continuum
kw: dependence · independence · interdependence
Covey organizes the seven habits along a path of maturity, which he presents as a three-stage continuum.

**Dependence.** This is everyone's starting point: as children, we depend entirely on others. The dependent person thinks in terms of "you": you take care of me, you are responsible for what happens to me, it's your fault if things go wrong. They need others to get what they want.

**Independence.** The independent person thinks in terms of "I": I can do it, I am responsible, I can choose. They no longer depend on others for their worth and their results. Our culture places great value on independence, and many personal development books stop there.

**Interdependence.** The interdependent person thinks in terms of "we": we can do it, we can cooperate, we can combine our talents to create something greater. Covey argues that interdependence is a more mature stage than independence. Life is by nature interdependent: you cannot succeed alone in a marriage, a family, a team or an organization.

Covey makes an important point: you cannot skip the stage of independence. A dependent person cannot become interdependent. They lack the inner strength to cooperate as an equal. They risk losing themselves in relationships or becoming trapped by them.

The seven habits follow this path. The first three concern the **private victory**: the move from dependence to independence, through self-mastery. The next three concern the **public victory**: the move from independence to interdependence, through cooperation with others. The seventh habit, renewal, sustains the other six.

Covey insists on the order: the private victory comes before the public victory. You cannot build solid relationships of trust without first building yourself.

> À retenir | Dependence, independence, interdependence. You cannot cooperate fully with others before you have won self-mastery.

--- Production and production capability
kw: goose that laid the golden eggs · balance
Covey defines effectiveness through a famous fable: the goose that laid the golden eggs. One day a farmer discovers that one of his geese lays a golden egg. Every day it lays another, and the farmer grows rich. But he also grows impatient. He decides to kill the goose to get all the eggs at once. He then finds it empty. He has lost both the eggs and the goose.

Covey uses this fable to define effectiveness as a balance between two elements: **production**, that is, the desired results, the golden eggs; and **production capability**, that is, what makes it possible to produce those results, the goose. If you focus only on production, you exhaust the production capability, and the results eventually disappear. If you focus only on production capability, you produce nothing.

He gives several examples of this balance:

- **For a machine**: if you run it without ever maintaining it, it will produce a lot at first, then break down.
- **For the body**: if you work non-stop without sleep or exercise, you get short-term results, then burn out.
- **For a relationship**: if you keep demanding more of a partner without ever investing in the relationship, it deteriorates.
- **For an organization**: if a leader pushes their teams to produce ever more without investing in their training and well-being, results eventually fall. The same goes for customers: a company that neglects customer satisfaction to maximize short-term profits loses its customers.

Covey argues that this balance lies at the heart of all the habits. Each aims to produce results while preserving and developing the capability to produce them. The seventh habit, renewal, is devoted entirely to production capability.

> Question clé | Which "goose that lays the golden eggs" are you wearing out right now: your health, a relationship, a team, a customer?

=== Be proactive, begin with the end
--- Habit 1: be proactive
kw: freedom to choose · responsibility
The first habit is the foundation of all the others. Being proactive means taking responsibility for your own life. Covey argues that between what happens to us and how we respond, there is a space. In that space lies our freedom to choose our response.

He illustrates this idea with the story of Viktor Frankl, an Austrian psychiatrist deported to the Nazi concentration camps. Frankl lost almost his entire family and endured inhuman conditions. Yet he discovered that there remained one freedom no one could take from him: the freedom to choose his attitude toward what was happening to him. This inner freedom helped him survive and give meaning to his experience, which he later described in a famous book.

Covey contrasts two attitudes. **Reactive** people let themselves be ruled by circumstances: the weather, other people's moods, events. If the weather is good, they feel good; if it rains, they are gloomy. If they are treated well, they feel well; if not, they feel bad. **Proactive** people act according to their values, whatever the circumstances. They feel emotions, but they do not let emotions decide for them.

Covey stresses that language reveals how proactive we are. Reactive people say: "there's nothing I can do," "that's just the way I am," "he drives me crazy," "I have to." Proactive people say: "let's look at our alternatives," "I can choose a different approach," "I control my own feelings," "I choose." Changing your language is a first way to become more proactive.

He specifies that proactivity is not naive optimism or busyness. It is not denying difficulties or forcing things. It is recognizing that you always have some room to choose, however small, and using it.

> Exercice | For one day, note each time you say or think "I have to," "I have no choice" or "he annoys me." Rephrase each sentence in terms of choice.

--- Influence and concern
kw: what I control · what worries me
To make proactivity concrete, Covey offers a visual tool: two concentric circles.

The large circle, the **circle of concern**, contains everything that concerns or worries us: our health, our children, problems at work, the economy, politics, the climate. The small circle inside it is the **circle of influence**: it contains the items in the circle of concern that we can actually do something about.

Reactive people focus their energy on the circle of concern, outside the circle of influence. They talk about other people's faults, problems in the environment, circumstances over which they have no control. This negative energy has a paradoxical effect: their circle of influence shrinks. By complaining and blaming others, they lose trust and the ability to act.

Proactive people focus their energy on the circle of influence. They act on what they can change. This positive energy has the opposite effect: their circle of influence grows. By acting effectively on what they control, they gain others' trust and the ability to influence more.

Covey distinguishes three types of problems:

- **Direct control problems**, which involve our own behaviour. We solve them by changing our habits.
- **Indirect control problems**, which involve other people's behaviour. We solve them by changing our methods of influence.
- **No control problems**, such as the past or the constraints of the situation. We solve them by changing our attitude: by accepting them with serenity.

He also suggests distinguishing two kinds of sentences: the "haves" and the "bes." Sentences from the circle of concern are often "haves": if only I had a better boss, more time, more money. Sentences from the circle of influence are "bes": I can be more patient, more organized, more competent.

> À retenir | Focus your energy on what you can influence, and your influence grows. Focusing it on the rest shrinks it.

--- Habit 2: begin with the end in mind
kw: eulogy · what really matters
The second habit means starting everything with a clear picture of the desired result. Covey first applies it to life as a whole.

He proposes a striking exercise. Imagine you are attending a funeral. As you enter, you discover it is your own, three years from now. Four people are going to speak: a family member, a friend, a work colleague and someone from an association or community you belong to. What would you want them to say about you? What qualities, what contributions, what achievements would you want them to mention?

Covey argues that this exercise reveals what really matters to us, beyond the urgencies of daily life. Many people discover a large gap between what they would want said about them and how they actually live. You can climb very high on the ladder of success and discover, once at the top, that it was leaning against the wrong wall.

He explains that all things are created twice. The first creation is mental: the architect draws the plans before building the house; the entrepreneur imagines the business before founding it. The second creation is physical: the concrete realization. If we do not consciously make the first creation, others make it for us: circumstances, habits, other people's expectations.

Covey also distinguishes **leadership** from **management**. Leadership corresponds to the first creation: deciding on the direction. Management corresponds to the second: organizing action to move in that direction. He offers an image: the efficient manager is the one who moves the team through the jungle, cutting through the undergrowth as fast as possible; the leader is the one who climbs the tallest tree, looks around and shouts "wrong jungle!"

- All things are created twice: first in the mind, then in reality.
- Check that your ladder is leaning against the right wall.
- Leadership chooses the direction; management organizes the path.

--- Personal mission statement and roles
kw: principles · centre · life roles
To apply the second habit, Covey recommends writing a **personal mission statement**. This is a text, often a page long, that describes what you want to be, what you want to do and the principles on which you want to base your life. Covey compares it to a personal constitution: a reference text that guides every important decision.

He specifies that a mission statement is not written in one evening. It requires deep reflection, often over several weeks, and should be revised regularly. But the process of writing it is as important as the result: it forces you to think about what really matters.

Covey insists on the **centre** of our life, that is, what serves as our source of security, guidance, wisdom and power. Many people centre their lives on one particular thing: spouse, family, money, work, possessions, pleasure, friends, a church or themselves. Each of these centres carries risks. If you centre your life on your work, you are lost when you lose it. If you centre it on a spouse, you become dependent on their moods. Covey recommends centring your life on **principles**, which do not change and offer a stable foundation.

He also suggests thinking about your **roles**. Each of us has several roles in life: parent, spouse, manager, colleague, friend, member of an association. For each role, you can ask what you want to accomplish and how you want to be seen. This approach helps balance your life and prevents one role, often work, from absorbing all the others.

The second habit also applies to organizations. Covey recommends that every organization have a written mission statement, developed with the participation of its members. A mission imposed from above commits no one. A mission built together creates commitment.

> Exercice | Do the funeral exercise, then write a first draft of your personal mission statement in half a page, covering your main roles.

=== First things first
--- Habit 3: the time management matrix
kw: urgent · important · four quadrants
The third habit puts the first two into practice. The first says: I am responsible. The second says: this is what I want. The third says: I do what matters first. This is the domain of personal management, discipline and will.

Covey offers a tool that has become famous: the time management matrix. It crosses two criteria, urgency and importance, to form four quadrants.

- **Quadrant I: urgent and important.** Crises, pressing problems, deadline-driven projects. They must be dealt with, but a life spent in this quadrant leads to stress and burnout.
- **Quadrant II: important but not urgent.** Planning, prevention, relationship building, training, health, reflection. This is the quadrant of effectiveness. These activities do not press on us, which is why we neglect them.
- **Quadrant III: urgent but not important.** Some interruptions, some calls, some meetings, many messages. These activities seem important because they are urgent, but they mostly serve other people's priorities.
- **Quadrant IV: neither urgent nor important.** Pure distraction, time-wasters.

Covey observes that ineffective people spend their time in quadrants I and III, then try to unwind in quadrant IV. Effective people shrink quadrants III and IV and spend more time in quadrant II. Paradoxically, this also shrinks quadrant I: by planning, preventing and building relationships, you avoid many crises.

The difficulty is that quadrant II requires initiative. No one forces you to exercise, prepare a plan or nurture a relationship. You have to decide to. That is why the third habit rests on the first two: you can say no to urgencies only if you have a stronger "yes," that is, a clear vision of what matters.

> À retenir | Effectiveness is decided in what is important but not urgent. To give it time, you have to be able to say no to other people's urgencies.

--- Planning your week
kw: roles · big rocks · flexibility
Covey offers a concrete method for living in quadrant II: weekly planning. He prefers the week to the day, because a day is too short to balance your different roles, whereas a week offers a fuller view.

The method follows four steps:

- **Identify your roles.** Write down your main roles for the week: for example, parent, spouse, manager, project leader, friend, and yourself as an individual.
- **Select goals.** For each role, choose one or two important goals for the week, preferably from quadrant II. For example: prepare the quarterly plan, spend an evening with your children, do three workouts.
- **Schedule.** Put these goals into the week's calendar at specific times, before filling in the rest. These are the "big rocks" that must go into the jar before the sand.
- **Adapt daily.** Each day, review the plan and adjust it to the unexpected, without losing sight of priorities.

Covey insists on flexibility. The plan is not a straitjacket. If a friend is going through a crisis, it is normal to set a goal aside to help them. The relationship is more important than the plan. The aim is not to follow your calendar to the letter, but to make sure what really matters finds its place in it.

He also stresses that this kind of planning is centred on people and principles, not just on tasks. Classic time management methods focus on efficiency: doing more things in less time. Covey argues that with people, efficiency is often ineffective. You cannot quickly solve a relationship problem or raise a child in a few minutes. With things you can be efficient; with people you must be effective.

- Plan by the week, by role, starting with quadrant II.
- Put the big rocks in before everything else.
- With people, effectiveness comes before efficiency.

--- Delegating for real
kw: results · "green and clean"
Covey argues that delegation is the most powerful way to increase your capacity. He distinguishes two forms of delegation.

**Gofer delegation.** You tell the other person what to do and how to do it, step by step: go get this, do that, tell me when it's done. This form of delegation is not very effective, because the delegator has to supervise every step. It limits how many people you can manage and does not develop others' skills.

**Stewardship delegation.** You agree on the expected results and let the other person choose the means. This form of delegation takes more time at the start, but then frees up a lot of time and helps people grow.

Covey describes five elements to clarify in stewardship delegation:

- **Desired results**: what is expected, not how to do it.
- **Guidelines**: the limits to respect, the mistakes to avoid, without describing every step.
- **Resources**: the human, financial and technical means available.
- **Accountability**: the criteria for success and the times when you will review progress.
- **Consequences**: what will happen in case of success or failure.

He illustrates this method with a story about his seven-year-old son. He wanted to hand him responsibility for the yard. Rather than giving him a list of tasks, he explained the desired result by showing him the neighbour's yard: a "green and clean" yard. He let him choose how to get there. He specified what he must not do, the tools available and that they would walk around the yard together twice a week. For the first few days the boy did nothing. Covey resisted the temptation to take the job back. During one walk around the yard, the boy finally understood that it really was his responsibility, and he took care of it carefully.

> Exercice | Choose a task you delegate by detailing every step. Redefine it with the five elements: results, guidelines, resources, accountability, consequences.

=== Trust and win-win
--- The emotional bank account
kw: deposits · withdrawals · trust
Before presenting the habits of the public victory, Covey introduces a metaphor that underpins all relationships: the **emotional bank account**. Like a bank account, every relationship has a balance of trust. We make deposits when we act in ways that strengthen trust, and withdrawals when we erode it.

When the balance is high, communication is easy, fast and effective. You can make a mistake and the other person forgives you. You can say something clumsily and they understand your intention. When the balance is low, every word is interpreted with suspicion, and the slightest mistake sparks conflict.

Covey describes six major deposits:

- **Understanding the individual**: knowing what matters to them, not what would matter to us in their place.
- **Attending to the little things**: acts of kindness, signs of respect. In relationships, the little things are the big things.
- **Keeping commitments**: every promise kept is a deposit; every promise broken is a major withdrawal.
- **Clarifying expectations**: many conflicts come from implicit, unshared expectations.
- **Showing personal integrity**: being loyal to those who are absent. If you criticize someone behind their back, those present understand that you will do the same to them.
- **Apologizing sincerely** when you make a withdrawal. But Covey specifies that repeated apologies for the same fault lose their value.

He stresses that deposits take time, and that close relationships require constant deposits. A relationship with a teenager, a spouse or a close colleague deteriorates if it is not maintained, because expectations are higher.

For a manager, this metaphor is very useful. Before asking for extra effort or giving difficult feedback, ask yourself what the account balance is. If it is low, it is better to make deposits before making a withdrawal.

> À retenir | Every relationship has a balance of trust. Keep your commitments, be loyal to those who are absent and make deposits before you ask.

--- Habit 4: think win-win
kw: six paradigms · abundance mentality
The fourth habit is to seek, in every interaction, solutions that benefit all parties. Covey presents six paradigms of human interaction.

- **Win-win**: both parties are satisfied with the solution. This is the goal.
- **Win-lose**: I win, you lose. This is the paradigm of competition, often learned from childhood at school or in sport.
- **Lose-win**: I lose, you win. This is the paradigm of those who always give in to avoid conflict. It builds up frustration and resentment.
- **Lose-lose**: no one wins. This is often the result of two win-lose people clashing, or of someone who would rather the other lose even if they lose too.
- **Win**: I want to win, without caring about the other. This is the most common paradigm in everyday negotiations.
- **Win-win or no deal**: if we cannot find a solution that benefits everyone, we agree not to make a deal. Covey considers this the most mature paradigm, because it frees you from the pressure of having to close at any cost.

Covey specifies that win-win is not a negotiation technique. It is a philosophy of human relationships. It rests on three character traits: **integrity**, that is, fidelity to your values and commitments; **maturity**, that is, the balance between the courage to defend your interests and consideration for those of others; and the **abundance mentality**, the conviction that there is enough for everyone.

The abundance mentality is the opposite of the **scarcity mentality**, which sees life as a fixed-size pie: if someone takes a bigger slice, there is less left for me. People with a scarcity mentality find it hard to share credit, recognition or power. They find it hard to be genuinely happy about others' success.

- Win-win is a philosophy, not a technique.
- "No deal" is a legitimate option that relieves the pressure.
- The abundance mentality makes it possible to share credit and recognition.

--- Building win-win agreements
kw: agreements · systems · processes
Covey argues that win-win is not enough as an intention. It must be supported by five mutually reinforcing dimensions.

**Character**, already described: integrity, maturity and abundance mentality.

**Relationships.** Win-win is much easier when the emotional bank account is well funded. With someone who trusts us, we can look for a solution together. With someone who is suspicious, we must first build trust.

**Agreements.** Covey recommends formalizing win-win agreements, especially at work. He takes up the five elements of stewardship delegation: desired results, guidelines, resources, accountability and consequences. A clear agreement avoids misunderstandings and lets everyone know what they must do.

**Systems.** Covey stresses that you cannot ask people to cooperate if the organization's systems reward competition. He tells the story of a company that asked its salespeople to cooperate while displaying on the wall a ranking of their individual results, with a trip for the top performer. The system's message contradicted the speech. Pay, appraisal, training and communication systems must support win-win.

**Processes.** Covey proposes a four-step approach to reach a win-win solution: first, see the problem from the other person's point of view, genuinely seeking to understand their needs; next, identify the key issues and concerns, not the positions; then determine what results would make a fully acceptable solution; finally, look for options to achieve those results.

This approach applies in many situations: a negotiation with a supplier, a conflict between colleagues, a discussion with a teenager about curfew, a budget allocation between departments.

> Question clé | In your organization, do the reward systems encourage cooperation or competition? What do your people conclude from watching them?

=== Understand and create together
--- Habit 5: seek first to understand
kw: empathic listening · four responses
The fifth habit is the one Covey considers the key to communication: seek first to understand, then to be understood. He argues that most people do not listen with the intent to understand, but with the intent to reply. They filter what they hear through their own experience and prepare their answer while the other person is talking.

Covey describes five levels of listening. You can **ignore** the other person, not listening at all. You can **pretend** to listen, nodding along. You can listen **selectively**, hearing only certain parts. You can listen **attentively**, focusing on the words. Finally, you can listen **empathically**, that is, with the intent to understand the other person from their point of view, their feelings as much as their ideas.

He observes that we often react to what others say with four kinds of responses, which he calls autobiographical, because they start from our own experience:

- **Evaluating**: we agree or disagree.
- **Probing**: we ask questions from our own frame of reference.
- **Advising**: we give advice based on our own experience.
- **Interpreting**: we explain the other person's motives and behaviour based on our own.

These responses are not bad in themselves, but they come too soon. As long as the other person does not feel understood, they are not ready to hear them.

Empathic listening requires reflecting back what the other person says, not by repeating their words, but by expressing what you have understood of their feelings and point of view. For example: "you feel your efforts aren't being recognized, and that's discouraging you." When the other person feels truly understood, they relax, and the conversation can move forward.

Covey compares empathic listening to psychological air. When someone feels understood, it is as if they were allowed to breathe. They can then open up to other people's ideas.

> Exercice | In your next difficult conversation, forbid yourself any advice for the first ten minutes. Only reflect back what you understand of the other person's feelings.

--- Then, be understood
kw: credibility · emotion · logic
The second half of the fifth habit is making yourself understood. Covey insists that this takes courage, just as listening takes consideration. Once you have understood the other person, you must clearly express your own point of view.

He draws on classical rhetoric and its three levers: **ethos**, **pathos** and **logos**. Ethos is personal credibility, the trust inspired by the speaker, their character. Pathos is the emotional side: the ability to be in tune with the other person's emotions. Logos is logic, reasoning. Covey stresses that the order matters. Many people start with logic, presenting their arguments. But without credibility and an emotional connection, logic does not persuade.

He gives an example. An employee wants to propose a new project to their manager. If they start by presenting their arguments, the manager may reject them. If they start by showing that they understand the manager's concerns, priorities, constraints and fears, they create the conditions for the project to be heard. They can even present the project by showing how it addresses the manager's concerns.

Covey recommends presenting your ideas clearly, specifically and visually, starting from your understanding of the other person. He stresses that the fifth habit is not a persuasion technique. It rests on a sincere intention to understand, not on a strategy to get your ideas across.

He also observes that the fifth habit is within everyone's circle of influence. You cannot control how others listen to you, but you can always choose to listen to them first. Often, this attitude also changes how others listen to you in return.

- Have the courage to express your view after understanding the other person.
- Credibility, emotion, then logic: the order matters.
- Present your ideas starting from the other person's concerns.

--- Habit 6: synergize
kw: differences · third alternative · creativity
The sixth habit is the culmination of the previous ones. Synergy means creating, together, solutions better than those each person would have found alone. The whole is greater than the sum of its parts.

Covey argues that synergy rests on valuing differences. When two people have different points of view, they can react in several ways. They can defend themselves, each trying to impose their view. They can compromise, each giving up part of what they wanted. Or they can look for a **third alternative**, a new solution that integrates both people's needs and goes beyond them.

He takes the example of a couple planning their holiday. The husband would like to take the children to a lake to go fishing, as he has been looking forward to for months. His wife would like to visit her mother, who is ill and lives far away. Each has good reasons. They could clash, with one giving in reluctantly, or split the difference and have a frustrating holiday. Covey shows that by taking the time to deeply understand each other's needs, they can find a new solution: for example, a stay near the mother's home, in a place that also offers activities for the children, or another time for the visit that makes it more helpful.

Covey stresses that synergy requires a high level of trust and communication. When the emotional bank account is high and each person truly listens to the other, differences become a source of creativity. When trust is low, differences become a source of conflict.

He describes three levels of communication. **Defensive** communication, based on mistrust, protects each party but produces nothing. **Respectful** communication, based on politeness, avoids conflict but often ends in compromise. **Synergistic** communication, based on strong trust, produces new solutions.

For a manager, synergy applies to meetings, projects and conflicts. It requires seeing disagreements as opportunities, taking the time to understand each point of view, and looking together for a solution no one had imagined at the outset.

> À retenir | Faced with a disagreement, look for a third alternative rather than a compromise. Differences are a resource when trust is present.

=== Sharpen the saw
--- Habit 7: the four dimensions
kw: physical · mental · social · spiritual
The seventh habit is the one that makes all the others possible. Covey illustrates it with a story. A man has been sawing down a tree for hours. He is exhausted and making slower and slower progress. A passer-by asks why he doesn't take a few minutes to sharpen his saw. The man replies that he doesn't have time: he's too busy sawing.

Sharpening the saw means taking time to renew yourself. It is the direct application of the principle of production capability: preserving and developing the goose that lays the golden eggs. Covey distinguishes four dimensions of renewal.

- **The physical dimension**: taking care of your body through exercise, a balanced diet, sleep and stress management. Covey recommends at least thirty minutes of exercise several times a week, combining endurance, flexibility and strength.
- **The mental dimension**: continuing to learn, read, write and think. Covey observes that many people stop learning after their studies. He recommends reading regularly, limiting time spent in front of entertainment screens and writing to clarify your thinking.
- **The social and emotional dimension**: nurturing your relationships, making deposits in emotional bank accounts, practising the habits of the public victory every day.
- **The spiritual dimension**: reconnecting with your values and what gives your life meaning. For some, this means prayer or meditation; for others, nature, music, literature. Covey recommends regularly rereading your personal mission statement.

Covey argues that these four dimensions must be balanced. Neglecting one weakens the others. He recommends spending time every day renewing each of them, which he calls the daily private victory. One hour a day, in his view, is enough to transform your life in the long run.

He stresses that these activities all belong to quadrant II: they are important but rarely urgent. That is why they are so easily neglected.

> Question clé | Which of the four dimensions have you neglected most over the last three months? What small regular action could you start this week?

--- The upward spiral
kw: learn · commit · do
Covey ends the book by presenting renewal as an upward spiral. Personal development does not follow a straight line, but a repeated cycle: learn, commit, do, then learn again at a higher level. Each turn of the spiral moves us forward.

He stresses that conscience plays an essential role in this spiral. Conscience is the inner voice that tells us whether we are true to our principles. For it to stay clear, it must be educated and listened to. When we act against our conscience, its voice grows fainter. When we act in harmony with it, it grows stronger and more precise.

Covey also links the seven habits together. He presents them as a system in which each habit reinforces the others. Being proactive makes it possible to define a mission. The mission makes it possible to prioritize. The private victory makes trust possible, which makes win-win, empathic listening and synergy possible. Renewal sustains the whole.

He insists on patience. Habits do not take hold in a few days. It takes months, sometimes years. Covey compares changing habits to a rocket leaving the atmosphere: most of the energy is spent at lift-off, to break free of gravity. Once in orbit, movement becomes easier. Likewise, the first efforts to change a habit are the hardest.

Covey also specifies that a habit lies at the intersection of three elements: knowledge, that is, knowing what to do and why; skill, that is, knowing how to do it; and desire, that is, wanting to do it. All three are needed for a habit to take hold. Many people know what they should do but have neither the skill nor the desire to do it.

Finally, Covey reminds us that the seven habits are not a formula for quick success. They are principles for living, which apply at work, in the family and in the community. Their aim is not just effectiveness, but a richer and more coherent life.

- Learn, commit, do: a cycle that repeats at a higher level.
- A habit requires knowledge, skill and desire.
- The first efforts cost the most, like a rocket's lift-off.

--- Renewing a team
kw: rhythm · training · recovery
Covey also applies the seventh habit to organizations. A team, like a person, has a production capability that must be maintained. A team that moves from project to project without ever taking time to breathe, learn or take stock eventually burns out, even if its results are good for a while.

He describes the four dimensions of renewal at the level of an organization. The **physical** dimension corresponds to material and financial resources, but also to people's health and workload. The **mental** dimension corresponds to training, skills development and reflection on practices. The **social and emotional** dimension corresponds to the quality of relationships, trust and how people are treated. The **spiritual** dimension corresponds to meaning: the organization's mission, values and contribution.

For a manager, this translates into a few simple practices:

- **Plan breathing space** after intense periods, rather than moving straight on to the next project.
- **Hold lessons-learned reviews** at the end of each project, to learn from what worked and what didn't.
- **Set aside time for training**, even when the workload is high.
- **Watch for signs of burnout**: absences, irritability, falling quality, departures.
- **Regularly remind the team of its mission** and show each person how their work contributes to it.

Covey stresses that these practices all belong to quadrant II. They are never urgent, which is why they are so often sacrificed. But a team that does not renew itself eventually faces crises, departures and declining quality, which cost far more than the time invested.

> À retenir | A team has its own goose that lays the golden eggs too. Schedule time for recovery, learning and meaning before it becomes urgent.

=== Putting it into practice
--- 30-day action plan
kw: one habit at a time · typical week
The seven habits form a programme for life, not a one-month plan. But you can start building them gradually. Here is a four-week proposal.

**Week 1: proactivity.** Notice your reactive language and rephrase it in terms of choice. Draw your two circles: concern and influence. For three issues that worry you, identify what lies within your circle of influence and one concrete action you can take.

**Week 2: the end in mind.** Do the funeral exercise. Write a first draft of your personal mission statement in half a page. List your main roles and, for each, what you want to accomplish this year.

**Week 3: first things first.** On Sunday evening, plan the week by role. For each role, choose a quadrant II goal and put it in your calendar. Identify one quadrant III task to cut back. Choose one delegation and redefine it with the five elements.

**Week 4: relationships and renewal.** Choose an important relationship and make three deposits in the emotional bank account. In a difficult conversation, practise empathic listening for ten minutes before giving your view. For an ongoing disagreement, look for a third alternative. Finally, set aside a moment each day for one of the four dimensions of renewal.

After this first month, Covey recommends starting the cycle again, deepening each habit. He also advises teaching the habits to others: explaining an idea to someone is the best way to internalize it.

> À retenir | One habit a week to begin with, then a cycle that repeats. Teaching the habits to others helps you internalize them.

--- Self-assessment grid
kw: seven habits · fourteen questions
Rate each statement from 1 (not at all true) to 5 (completely true).

**Habit 1: proactivity**

- I focus on what I can influence rather than on what I don't control.
- I talk about my choices rather than my constraints.

**Habit 2: end in mind**

- I have a clear idea of what I want to accomplish in my different roles.
- My important decisions are guided by explicit values.

**Habit 3: first things first**

- Every week I spend time on activities that are important but not urgent.
- I delegate by defining results rather than tasks.

**Habit 4: win-win**

- In negotiations, I look for a solution that satisfies both parties.
- I am genuinely glad about other people's success.

**Habit 5: seek first to understand**

- I listen to understand, not to prepare my reply.
- People feel understood when they talk to me.

**Habit 6: synergy**

- I see disagreements as a source of better ideas.
- I look for third alternatives rather than compromises.

**Habit 7: renewal**

- I regularly take care of my physical health.
- I set aside time to learn and recharge.

**Interpretation.** Look first at the first three habits. If they are weak, start there: Covey insists that the private victory comes before the public victory. If they are solid but habits 4 to 6 are weak, work on trust and listening. If the seventh is weak, all the others are threatened in the long run.

- The private victory comes before the public victory.
- Neglected renewal threatens all the other habits.

--- Case study: the overwhelmed manager
kw: urgencies · family · team
Mehdi leads a team of fifteen technicians at an industrial maintenance company. He is competent and well liked, but overwhelmed. His days are filled with emergencies, he works late, sleeps badly and rarely sees his children. His team complains that they can never talk to him.

**Proactivity.** Mehdi draws his circles. He discovers that he spends a lot of energy complaining about senior management, which sets unrealistic targets, and about customers, who call at all hours. These issues belong to his circle of concern. He decides to focus on what he can influence: how his team is organized and how he himself works.

**The end in mind.** The funeral exercise shakes him. He would like his children to say he was there for them, and his team to say he helped them grow. He realizes he is far from both goals.

**First things first.** Analysing his time, he finds that most of his days are spent in quadrants I and III. He handles urgent breakdowns himself because he is the best technician. He decides to delegate call-outs to two experienced technicians, clearly defining the expected results and the cases in which they should call him. Every week he schedules quadrant II time: a one-to-one with each technician, preparation of a preventive maintenance plan, and two evenings with his children.

**Relationships.** He practises empathic listening in the one-to-ones. One technician confides that he is thinking of leaving for lack of prospects. Together they find a third alternative: a role as the go-to expert on a new type of equipment.

**Renewal.** Mehdi starts cycling again twice a week.

Three months later, the number of emergencies has fallen thanks to preventive maintenance. Mehdi gets home earlier two evenings a week.

> Question de réflexion | Which emergency do you handle yourself when a member of your team could take it on?

=== Critical view and links
--- Limits of the book
kw: obviousness · religion · individualism
*The 7 Habits* is a highly influential book, but it also has its limits.

**Ideas that are sometimes obvious.** Several critics have pointed out that many of the book's principles are common sense: listen to others, keep your commitments, look after your health. Covey acknowledges this himself, arguing that these principles are universal and ancient. The book's value lies mainly in organizing them into a coherent system and offering tools to apply them.

**A religious influence.** Covey was a committed member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. The book does not proselytize, but some ideas, such as the existence of universal principles and the place of the spiritual dimension, bear the mark of this conviction. Some readers see this as a limitation, others as an enrichment.

**A very individual view.** The book emphasizes individual responsibility. This can feel guilt-inducing for people living in very difficult situations: precarity, illness, discrimination, alienating work. The circle of influence is real, but it is not the same size for everyone. Proactivity should therefore be read as an invitation to use whatever room you have, not as a denial of structural constraints.

**Little empirical evidence.** Like many books of this kind, it relies on the author's experience, anecdotes and reflections rather than rigorous studies. Some ideas have been confirmed by research, such as the importance of sleep and exercise, or the benefits of active listening. Others remain convictions.

**A long and sometimes dense book.** The book is long and some passages are repetitive. It is best read in stages, one habit at a time, rather than in one go.

**A dated planning method.** The planning tools described are based on paper planners. They remain valid but need to be adapted to today's digital tools and constant interruptions.

- The system is more valuable than any single idea taken in isolation.
- Proactivity should not make you forget real constraints.

--- The sequel: finding your voice
kw: eighth habit · contribution
Fifteen years after *The 7 Habits*, Covey published a new book devoted to what he calls the eighth habit. In it he explains that the world of work has changed profoundly since 1989. The knowledge economy has taken over, and organizations need everyone to bring their full intelligence, creativity and commitment.

The eighth habit is to find your own voice, then to help others find theirs. For Covey, your voice lies at the intersection of talent, passion, need and conscience. It thus echoes Collins's three circles and Sinek's why.

He puts more emphasis on the leader's role: no longer just being effective yourself, but creating the conditions for everyone to contribute. He describes several roles of the leader, such as modelling, setting a clear direction, aligning systems with values and empowering people. Several ideas from the seven habits reappear in these roles, applied to leading others.

- Personal effectiveness prepares you to help others contribute.
- Your "voice" combines talent, passion, need and conscience.

--- Links with other books
kw: Drucker · Scott · Sinek · Goldsmith
**With The Effective Executive (Drucker).** The third habit, "first things first," almost exactly echoes the title of one of Drucker's chapters. Both authors insist on concentration, time management and delegation. Covey adds a dimension of personal values and balance between life's different roles.

**With Radical Candor (Kim Scott).** Covey's emotional bank account explains why radical candor works: caring personally makes deposits that then allow you to challenge directly. The empathic listening of the fifth habit matches the way Kim Scott recommends asking for and receiving feedback.

**With Start with Why (Sinek).** Covey's personal mission statement and Sinek's why are two expressions of the same idea: acting from what you deeply believe. Covey applies it mainly to the individual, Sinek to the organization.

**With What Got You Here Won't Get You There (Goldsmith).** Goldsmith shows how interpersonal behaviours hold back successful people. Several of these behaviours, such as not listening, needing to be right at all costs or failing to say thank you, correspond to withdrawals from the emotional bank account. Goldsmith offers a very concrete method for change that complements Covey's vision well.

**With The Five Dysfunctions of a Team (Lencioni).** Covey's synergy corresponds to the top of Lencioni's pyramid: a team that debates openly, commits and focuses on collective results. The private victory prepares each member for this cooperation.

> Parcours conseillé | Next, read What Got You Here Won't Get You There to move from principles to concrete changes in behaviour.
